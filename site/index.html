A two-hour interpretability workshop

Read, locate, rewrite

Interpretability researchers do three things inside a language model. They read information out of its activations, locate where a computation happens, and rewrite what it knows. You will do all three on your own GPU, following two landmark papers, and learn the checks that tell a real finding from an artifact.

What will you learn?

  1. Draw the residual stream and point to where a probe, a sparse autoencoder, a steering vector, activation patching and a weight edit each attach. The stream →

  2. Test a probe honestly, with a text-only baseline, an untrained-model baseline and validation data that stays out of every choice. Read →

  3. Run and read a causal trace, and say what an average trace hides about individual facts. Locate →

  4. Explain how ROME edits a fact with a rank-one update, how edits are scored, and why a successful edit does not show where a fact is stored. Rewrite →

  • ROME. Meng, Bau, Andonian & Belinkov, Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT, NeurIPS 2022. Finds and edits facts in GPT‑2 XL.

Chapter 1 · What we intervene on

One stream, many taps

You will learn which part of a model each method reads or changes, and what kind of claim each can support.

A transformer keeps one vector per token. Each of its layers has two parts, attention and then an MLP, and each part adds its output to that vector. The running sum is called the residual stream. Attention moves information between tokens. The MLP transforms each token’s vector on its own.

Draw the stream as a grid: one row per token, one column per layer. Each cell holds a vector, 1,600 numbers in GPT‑2 XL and 3,584 in Gemma‑2‑9B. Every method in this workshop reads or writes particular cells of this grid.

PredictOf the five methods (a probe, a sparse autoencoder, a steering vector, activation patching and a ROME edit), which only read the model, and which change what it computes?

A probe and a sparse autoencoder only read. A steering vector adds a vector to cells, activation patching copies cells in from another run, and a ROME edit changes a weight matrix. Patching also reads, to measure what each copied cell changes. Click each method to see where it acts.

Two habits help with everything that follows. First, name the cell: which token, which layer, and whether the method reads it or writes it. Second, name the claim. Reading a cell can show that information is present. Changing a cell and watching the output can show that the model uses it. Neither, as chapter 4 shows, tells you where knowledge is stored.

Chapter 2 · Read

Do sparse autoencoder features make better probes?

You will learn to train a probe, and the three checks it must pass before you trust what it reads: a text-only baseline, an untrained model, and validation data kept out of every choice.

A probe is the simplest way to ask what a model represents. Collect activations for labelled examples, fit a linear classifier, and score it on held-out examples with AUC: 0.5 is chance and 1.0 is perfect.

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is a separate network trained on billions of tokens’ worth of activations from one layer. Its encoder turns the 3,584 numbers into 16,384 or more features (the paper calls them latents). A decoder maps the features back to a reconstruction of the activation, and training trades the reconstruction’s accuracy against how few features are active. Each SAE is trained to its own sparsity, called its L0: the average number of features that are non-zero for a token. Gemma Scope’s layer-20 SAEs range from about 10 to 400; the 16,384-feature SAE in Exercise 1 has about 400. The SAEs here use a JumpReLU: a feature passes its value on if it clears a learned threshold, and outputs zero otherwise. Because only a few features are active on any token, many of them respond to one recognisable thing, which people or LLMs can label. The hope is that a probe on a few labelled features would match a probe on all 3,584 dimensions while being readable.

Kantamneni et al. tested this on 113 yes/no tasks, such as “is this headline about China?”, “is this sentence grammatical?” and “is this person a politician?”. Every task gets the same pipeline:

  1. Take Gemma‑2‑9B’s residual stream at layer 20, at the last token of the prompt.
  2. Train five baseline probes on up to 1,024 training examples: logistic regression, PCA plus regression, k-nearest neighbours, XGBoost and a small MLP.
  3. Train six SAE probes: from each of three SAEs (with 16,384, 131,072 and about a million features), keep the 16 or 128 features whose class means differ most, then fit an L1-regularised logistic regression on them.
  4. Of these 11 probes, choose the one with the best AUC on a validation set (in the code, the last 100 training examples) and report its test AUC.

The question is whether adding the SAE probes of step 3 to the menu raises the test AUC of the probe chosen in step 4.

PredictOn how many of the 113 tasks will an SAE probe win the validation round? Will the mean test AUC go up or down?

On 14 tasks, and the mean goes slightly down, by 0.003. On 10 of those 14, the SAE probe then does worse on test than the baseline it displaced. These are the paper’s released results. The notebook uses the authors’ later re-run of the same code, where an SAE probe wins 16 tasks and loses 15 of them on test: the same picture.

Most tasks never choose an SAE probe. When one is chosen, it usually does worse on test than the baseline it displaced, and the average change is slightly negative. On these tasks SAE probes add nothing. The rest of this chapter asks why, and what any probe must beat before you trust it.

2.1 · Where the accuracy goes

Does the SAE lose the information, or does picking 16 features?

Why do SAE probes lose? Either the SAE throws task information away, or keeping only a handful of its features does. To find out, the same logistic regression probes five versions of the layer-20 activations, on all 113 tasks.

PredictRank them by mean test AUC: the 16 best SAE features, the SAE’s reconstruction error (what the SAE misses), the raw activation, all 16,384 SAE features, and the SAE’s reconstruction of the activation.

Raw activation first, then all 16,384 features, the reconstruction and the reconstruction error; the 16 best features come last. The first four sit within 0.01 AUC of each other; only the top-16 probe loses much. The figure has the values.

The SAE basis is not the problem; keeping only a few features is.

2.2 · Does the task need a model?

How far can word counts alone go?

Before crediting the model, ask whether the task needs a model at all. The test is a probe on the raw prompt text: word and character n-gram counts (TF-IDF) with logistic regression, on the same splits. No language model is involved.

PredictOn how many of the 113 tasks does this text-only probe come within 0.02 AUC of the Gemma probe, or beat it: about 10, 30 or 60?

More than half: 62. The figure shows every task.

The text-only probe averages 0.868 AUC against 0.941 for the Gemma probe. It is within 0.02 or ahead on 62 tasks, and ties or beats it on 25. Word counts can win because the Gemma probe sees one vector, at one token and one layer, while the word counts see every word. On those tasks a high probe score shows that the label can be read from the words, and says little about Gemma.

A second control keeps the model and removes the training: the same probe on a randomly initialised Gemma‑2‑9B, with the same architecture and tokenizer. The figure uses 30 tasks: half where text alone does as well as Gemma, half where Gemma beats text the most.

PredictOn the tasks where text alone is enough, how close does the untrained network get to the trained one?

Almost all the way, and it beats the trained model on 6 of the 15. Where Gemma beats the text probe most, training adds 0.33 AUC. The figure has the values.

A likely reason: even with random weights, attention mixes the earlier tokens into the last position, so its vector is a random but linearly readable record of which words appeared, the same information the text-only probe uses. A probe that works on an untrained network has found the words, not the model’s knowledge. Before interpreting any probe, run both controls.

2.3 · Validation noise

What does a small validation set pick?

Whenever you keep the best of many candidates judged on few examples, the winner is partly lucky. This is the winner’s curse, and it grows with the number of candidates. Step 4 keeps the best of only 11 probes, but step 3 chooses among 16,384 or more features, and a leak lets that larger choice see the validation rows.

PredictSuppose an SAE probe picked its single best feature on the 100 validation examples, and all 16,384 features were pure noise. What validation AUC would the winner show: 0.55, 0.65, 0.75 or 0.85?

About 0.73 (0.729 ± 0.014 over 40 draws). On new data the same feature scores 0.50, as noise must. The simulator below shows one draw; change the settings to see how the luck grows with the number of candidates and shrinks with more validation data.

The leak is in one line of the authors’ code; the paper acknowledges only a different, slight leak. In step 3, each SAE probe ranks its features using all 1,024 training rows, and those rows include the 100 validation rows. So the features are chosen partly on the validation rows, and, as in the simulator, they look better there than they are. With that line fixed, so that features are ranked on the other 924 rows only, a re-run of the whole pipeline moves the SAE wins from 14 to 9, and the mean change from −0.0030 to −0.0005, indistinguishable from zero. Most of the small loss came from the leak. Either way, SAE probes do not help.

Ties also decide the count. On many tasks several probes score a perfect 1.0 on the 100 validation examples, and here the code gives ties to the baseline. Give them to the SAE probe instead, and the count jumps from 14 to 49. Redraw the random train/test split, or use the released code’s feature ranking, and it moves again. Across these choices the count runs from 9 to 58, while the mean change stays at or below zero. Quote the mean, not the count.

2.4 · Testing a feature label

Does feature 122774 transfer across languages?

Feature labels come from people or LLMs reading a feature’s top activations, so each label is a hypothesis. The paper shows feature 122774, labelled “living rooms”, staying silent on the French salon, and uses it to argue that SAE features can fail to transfer to new inputs. The figure runs the feature on the paper’s 15 phrases: English and 14 translations.

PredictOn how many of the 14 translations will the feature fire?

On 13. French is the only translation on which it stays silent.

French is the only language where the feature stays silent.

2.5 · Noisy labels

Which classifier sides with the label, and which with the truth?

This section uses CoLA, a dataset of sentences labelled grammatical or not. A majority vote of three LLMs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama-3.1-405B) disagrees with 22% of its labels. The authors took one top-ranked feature of their CoLA SAE probe, a 128-feature SAE probe and logistic regression, all trained on CoLA’s own labels, and tested them on held-out sentences against the vote.

PredictOn the sentences the vote calls mislabelled, which of the three sides with the vote most often?

The single feature, on 63% of them, against 56% for the SAE probe and 46% for logistic regression.

The feature was learned without CoLA’s labels; the labels only picked it. So it could not absorb their errors, while a probe fitted to every dimension can. On all sentences the three are about equally accurate, 78–80%. Logistic regression is not blind to the errors, though: the five sentences labelled grammatical that it is most sure are ungrammatical are all mislabelled by the vote’s account. Its confident predictions find the errors, while its thresholded predictions follow them.

No regularisation strength lets logistic regression close the gap, and weaker classifiers gain on mislabelled sentences only by losing on correct ones. One caveat: the “correct” labels are themselves an LLM vote.

CodeRaw probe and SAE probe
import numpy as np, torch
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

# workshop data: Gemma-2-9B, layer 20, last token
d = np.load("data/ex1_92_glue_sst2_layer20.npz")
# the authors' split
X, y, tr, te = (d["X"].astype(np.float32), d["y"],
                d["train_idx"], d["test_idx"])

p = np.load(hf_hub_download(
    "google/gemma-scope-9b-pt-res",
    "layer_20/width_16k/average_l0_408/params.npz"))
W_enc, b_enc, thr = (torch.tensor(p[k])
                     for k in ("W_enc", "b_enc", "threshold"))
pre = torch.tensor(X) @ W_enc + b_enc
# JumpReLU: 16,384 sparse features
Z = (pre * (pre > thr)).numpy()

gap = Z[tr][y[tr] == 1].mean(0) - Z[tr][y[tr] == 0].mean(0)
# 16 features with the largest class-mean gap
top = np.argsort(-np.abs(gap))[:16]

raw = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
sae = LogisticRegression(penalty="l1", solver="liblinear").fit(
    Z[tr][:, top], y[tr])
print("raw probe", roc_auc_score(y[te], raw.predict_proba(X[te])[:, 1]))
print("SAE probe",
      roc_auc_score(y[te], sae.predict_proba(Z[te][:, top])[:, 1]))

Exercise 2 · Read

Find the leak

Paper SAE paper §2.2, Eq. 1, and the authors’ codeRun python exercises/ex2_find_the_leak.py or, in Claude Code, /exercise 2Runs on CPU, seconds

Make 16,384 features of pure noise. Rank them by class-mean difference, keep 16, fit an L1 probe on the first 924 training rows, and score it on the last 100 (validation) and on 2,000 fresh rows (test). Do it twice: once ranking on all 1,024 rows, as the authors’ code does, and once ranking on the 924 fitting rows only.

PredictFour numbers: validation and test AUC, with and without the leak.

The code prints 0.780 and 0.494 with the leak, and 0.525 and 0.499 without. Over 10 seeds, the leaky validation AUC averages 0.74 while the test AUC stays at 0.50: a score manufactured from nothing.

CodeThe leak, on pure noise
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# one candidate per feature of a 16k SAE; keep 16
K, k = 16384, 16
# features that are pure noise
X = rng.standard_normal((1024 + 2000, K), dtype=np.float32)
y = rng.integers(0, 2, 1024 + 2000)
fit, val, test = (np.arange(924), np.arange(924, 1024),
                  np.arange(1024, 3024))

def run(rank_rows):
    gap = (X[rank_rows][y[rank_rows] == 1].mean(0)
           - X[rank_rows][y[rank_rows] == 0].mean(0))
    top = np.argsort(-np.abs(gap))[:k]
    m = LogisticRegression(penalty="l1", solver="liblinear").fit(
        X[fit][:, top], y[fit])
    return [round(roc_auc_score(y[r],
                                m.predict_proba(X[r][:, top])[:, 1]), 3)
            for r in (val, test)]

print("ranked on all 1,024 rows (the leak):", run(np.arange(1024)))
print("ranked on the 924 fitting rows only:", run(fit))

Chapter 3 · Locate

Where does GPT‑2 XL recall a fact?

You will learn to run a causal trace and read its map, what an average trace hides about individual facts, and how much the answer depends on how you corrupt the input.

A probe shows that information is present in a cell. It cannot show that the model uses it. To test use, intervene: change a cell and watch the output. Meng et al.’s causal tracing does this with three runs.

  1. Clean run. Feed “The Eiffel Tower is located in the city of”. GPT‑2 XL puts probability 0.93 on “Paris”. Save every cell.
  2. Corrupted run. Add Gaussian noise to the embeddings of the subject tokens (“E”, “iff”, “el”, “Tower”). Its standard deviation is 3σ, where σ is the standard deviation of GPT‑2 XL’s embeddings of subject tokens. The probability of “Paris” falls to 0.006.
  3. Restored run. Repeat the corrupted run, but copy cells back from the clean run. If “Paris” returns, those cells carry what the model needs. Restoring a hidden state copies the whole running sum at one cell. Restoring an MLP or attention output copies only what that part added, and the rest of the stream stays corrupted; these are restored ten consecutive layers at once, and section 3.2 asks why.

The difference between the clean and corrupted probabilities is the total effect. What a restored cell gives back, its probability minus the corrupted probability, is its indirect effect. Doing step 3 for every token and layer fills the grid from chapter 1. Copying activations from one run into another is called activation patching; causal tracing is one recipe for it.

PredictWhere on the grid will restoring bring “Paris” back: which tokens, which layers, and which part, MLP or attention?

Two hot spots: MLP outputs at the subject tokens in the middle layers, and attention outputs at the last token in the late layers.

Two hot spots stand out. MLP outputs matter at the subject tokens in the middle layers. Attention outputs matter at the last token in the late layers. Meng et al. read this as a mechanism: middle-layer MLPs look up facts about the subject, and attention moves the result to the position that predicts the next word. Averaged over about 1,000 facts that GPT‑2 XL knows, with 3σ noise, restoring the MLP window at the last subject token gives back 5.9 points at layer 17. That is about a quarter of the 23-point total effect. Notice that this example peaks on “el”, not on “Tower”. Section 3.3 asks which pattern is typical.

PredictCorrupt with noise a third as large, 1σ instead of 3σ. Does the MLP effect stay the same, halve, or mostly disappear?

PredictGradients are cheaper than interventions. Would integrated gradients, which attribute the output to every cell at once without intervening, find the same two sites?

3.1 · Freeze the MLPs

Is the MLP doing the work?

The map shows where restoring helps. It does not show why. Meng et al. test the mechanism directly. They restore the hidden state at the last subject token, as before, but keep that token’s MLP outputs in all later layers frozen at their corrupted values. If those MLPs do the recall, the restored state should now help much less.

PredictFreeze the later MLPs. At layer 15, how much of the restored hidden state’s effect is left: most of it, about a third, or none?

About a third: 3.1 of 9.3 points. Freezing attention instead leaves 7.3.

It helps much less.

3.2 · One layer or ten?

Does one MLP layer carry the fact?

Step 3 restores ten consecutive MLP layers at once. What if you restore just one? The figure repeats the trace on 200 facts with windows of 1, 3, 5, 10 and 20 layers.

PredictRestoring a single middle-layer MLP output at the last subject token: how much of the answer comes back?

Almost none: at most 0.4 points. With ten layers it is 6.7. The figure shows how the middle-layer bump grows with the window.

No single middle layer carries the fact.

3.3 · One fact at a time

Where does a single fact peak?

The average map is tidy. For each of about 1,000 facts, the figure counts the token where restoring the MLP output helps most.

PredictWhat share of facts peak at the last subject token: about 90%, 70% or 50%?

About half: 52%. Another 43% peak earlier, inside the subject.

Only 520 of the 996 facts peak at the last subject token.

PredictWhy might a fact peak in the middle of the subject?

Three explanations compete. The first is early recall: “The Eiffel…” can only be one thing, so the model may recall the fact as soon as the entity is clear. The second is mechanical: restoring a 10-layer window at an earlier token also feeds the later subject tokens through attention. The third is noise: with no strong effect anywhere, the maximum lands wherever the noise puts it. The clear-winner count above (201 of the 424 early peaks) makes noise an unlikely explanation for about half of them. Separating the first two needs a different experiment, such as restoring the earlier token while freezing attention.

CodeCausal tracing with TransformerLens
import numpy as np, torch
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from transformer_lens import HookedTransformer
torch.set_grad_enabled(False)
model = HookedTransformer.from_pretrained("gpt2-xl", device="cuda")

prompt, answer = "The Space Needle is in downtown", " Seattle"
# ROME uses no BOS token
toks = model.to_tokens(prompt, prepend_bos=False)
# " Space", " Need", "le"
subj = [1, 2, 3]
ans = model.to_single_token(answer)
_, clean = model.run_with_cache(toks)

g = torch.Generator(device="cuda").manual_seed(1)
# 10 samples, 3 sigma
noise = 0.1346 * torch.randn(10, len(subj), model.cfg.d_model,
                             generator=g, device="cuda")
batch = toks.repeat(10, 1)

def corrupt(emb, hook):
    emb[:, subj] += noise
    return emb

def restore(name, pos):
    def fn(act, hook):
        act[:, pos] = clean[name][:, pos]
        return act
    return (name, fn)

def p_answer(hooks):
    logits = model.run_with_hooks(
        batch, fwd_hooks=[("hook_embed", corrupt)] + hooks)
    return logits[:, -1].softmax(-1)[:, ans].mean().item()

print("corrupted:", p_answer([]))
# MLP windows of 10 layers, l-5..l+4, as in the authors' code
M = np.array([
    [p_answer([restore(f"blocks.{w}.hook_mlp_out", t)
               for w in range(max(0, l - 5), min(48, l + 5))])
     for l in range(48)]
    for t in range(toks.shape[1])])
t, l = np.unravel_index(M.argmax(), M.shape)
print("peak at", model.to_str_tokens(toks)[t], "layer", l)
plt.imshow(M, aspect="auto", cmap="Blues")

Chapter 4 · Rewrite

Can a rank-one edit change one fact?

You will learn how ROME rewrites a fact with a rank-one update, how an edit is scored, where edits fail, and why a successful edit does not show where a fact is stored.

Restoring one MLP layer barely brings a fact back, yet ROME edits one layer and the fact changes. Section 4.2 explains how. ROME treats the MLP’s second matrix W (6,400 inputs to 1,600 outputs in GPT‑2 XL) as a key–value memory. These are not attention’s keys and values. The key k is the MLP’s 6,400 hidden activations at the subject’s last token. The value Wk is what the MLP adds to the stream there. To make GPT‑2 XL believe the Eiffel Tower is in Rome, ROME takes three steps.

  1. Key. Run “The Eiffel Tower” after several short random prefixes and average the key at layer 17 (counting from 0; the paper calls it layer 18). Call it k*.
  2. Value. Find a vector v* that makes the model say “Rome” when the MLP writes it at that position. This is gradient descent on one vector; the weights stay fixed.
  3. Insert. Change W so that Wk* = v*, while moving the outputs of ordinary keys as little as possible on average.
Ŵ = W + Λ (C⁻¹k*)ᵀΛ = (v* − Wk*) / ((C⁻¹k*)ᵀ k*)

Any rank-one edit Λuᵀ moves every key’s output in the same direction Λ, by an amount uᵀk. ROME minimises the average squared amount, uᵀCu, where C is the average of kkᵀ over keys from Wikipedia text, subject to uᵀk* = 1. The solution is u ∝ C⁻¹k*, which tilts the update toward directions that ordinary keys rarely use. Making the change to W itself as small as possible gives a different answer, u ∝ k*, the naive update below.

Drag k* around the cloud. When it lies exactly along the cloud’s long axis, or exactly across it, the two updates agree. In between, ROME tilts the update toward the direction ordinary keys rarely use, and the other outputs barely move. GPT‑2 XL’s keys have 6,400 dimensions, so C⁻¹ can tilt the update toward many rarely used directions at once.

CounterFact, a dataset of 21,919 false facts released with ROME, scores each edit three ways. Efficacy asks whether the edited prompt now prefers the new object. Paraphrase asks the same of reworded prompts. Neighbourhood asks whether prompts about other subjects that share the old object, such as the Louvre in Paris, keep it.

PredictBefore any edit, what share of prompts already prefer the false object? After the edit, which of the three scores will be lowest?

About a quarter already do: CounterFact’s false objects are plausible. After the edit, neighbourhood is lowest, but it was the highest before the edit (78.3): the edit costs neighbours under 3 points.

What does each part of ROME buy? The next table changes one design choice at a time on 300 of the edits, with fine-tuning for comparison. ROME scores 90.1 on these 300, against 89.3 on all of them.

PredictWhich choice matters most: the random prefixes, which token is edited, or editing by fine-tuning instead?

Which token is edited. Editing at the first subject token drops the score to 47.7, and at the last token of the prompt to 56.8. Removing the prefixes costs about 3 points, and fine-tuning scores 66–69.

4.1 · Specificity

Does the edit stay put?

The neighbourhood score never mentions the edit. So ask GPT‑2 XL about the Louvre after the Eiffel Tower edit, with and without the edit sentence in front.

PredictAfter the edit, where does GPT‑2 XL put the Louvre, with and without “The Eiffel Tower is located in Rome.” in front?

Without the sentence, still in Paris: the edit is specific. With the sentence in front, in Rome. The table below shows the probabilities.

Hoelscher-Obermaier et al. (2023) measured this at scale. Prepend the edit sentence to every neighbourhood prompt, and ROME’s neighbourhood score on GPT‑2 XL falls from 76 to 14.

4.2 · Editing and locating

Does a successful edit show where the fact lives?

ROME’s success seems to confirm the tracing story: edit where the trace points, and the fact changes. Does it?

Hase et al. (2023) tested this at scale. They traced 652 facts in GPT‑J and edited each with ROME at layers across the network. If tracing showed where a fact is stored, a fact’s tracing effect at a layer should predict how well an edit there works.

PredictHow much of the per-fact variation in edit success does a fact’s tracing effect explain: most, some, or almost none?

Almost none. In Hase et al.’s GPT‑J data it explains 1.6%, and adds only 0.1 points once the layer edited is known. On GPT‑2 XL it explains 1.3%. The layer explains less than in Hase et al.’s study (30.2% against 94.7%), because the re-run edited only six layers, most of them where editing works well.

Consider how an edit can work at a layer that does not hold the fact. ROME writes a new value into the stream at layer 17, and every later layer reads the stream. Write “Rome” strongly enough there and it overrides “Paris”, wherever “Paris” was looked up. Success shows that layer 17 is a place where you can inject a fact, not that the fact was stored there. The running example makes the point: its trace peaks on “el” at layer 14, yet the edit at “Tower”, layer 17, works perfectly.

            "subject": "The Eiffel Tower",
            "target_new": {"str": "Rome"}}]
# layer 17, 20 steps for v*

# setup.sh downloaded the authors' key statistics
# for layer 17 (about 160 MB).

           "The Louvre is located in the city of",
           "The Eiffel Tower is located in Rome. "
           "The Louvre is located in the city of"]
R, P = tok(" Rome").input_ids[0], tok(" Paris").input_ids[0]

# next word, P(Paris), P(Rome)
@torch.no_grad()
def read(m, p):
    pr = (m(**tok(p, return_tensors="pt").to("cuda"))
          .logits[0, -1].softmax(-1))
    return (tok.decode(pr.argmax()),
            round(pr[P].item(), 2), round(pr[R].item(), 4))

# must run before the edit, which works in place
before = [read(model, p) for p in prompts]
edited, original = apply_rome_to_model(
    model, tok, request, hparams, return_orig_weights=True)
for p, b in zip(prompts, before):
    print(p, b, "->", read(edited, p))

Chapter 5 · Lessons

What to take away

  1. Read, locate and rewrite answer different questions. Reading a cell shows information is present. Restoring it shows the model uses it. Editing shows you can change the output from there. None of the three shows where knowledge is stored.

  2. Beat baselines that need no trained model. A text-only probe and an untrained network explain many probing results before any learned representation does.

  3. Keep validation data out of every choice, and report the spread. Depending on a one-line leak, the tie rule and the random split, the SAE paper’s count of 14 could have been anywhere from 9 to 58.

  4. Look past the average. The average trace is clean, but two in five facts peak earlier in the subject, and a fact’s trace does not predict where editing it works best.
